# Interim Progress Report — Questionnaire Answers

*Fill-in answers for the 23 questions. Each answer is a self-contained paragraph ready to paste into a form.*

**1. What specific problem or pain point does your project address?**

The project addresses the gap experienced by students who feel distressed but are not ready or able to seek human help. Some do not know where to start; others struggle to describe experiences built up over time from multiple events. Even when they know counselling services exist, they must first organise what to say and decide what to disclose. For example, a student may record academic, interpersonal and family events on different days, yet only say "I have been very stressed lately" at a first meeting. Unfold lets students record anything freely, while the system automatically organises timelines, content attributes and related topics into a support summary, reducing the preparation needed for a first conversation.

**2. What research have you conducted to better understand the problem?**

Our desk research draws on Hong Kong studies: HK-YES surveyed 3,340 residents aged 15–24 and found 74.1% of those with a probable mental disorder had received no services; another study of 945 university students found only about a quarter of those with moderate to severe depressive symptoms had sought professional help. These establish the severity of the help-seeking gap but not whether students would use our product. For primary research, we have designed a questionnaire around this product and problem — covering help-seeking barriers, recording preferences, privacy understanding and willingness to share — and it is now being distributed. Responses have not yet been collected or analysed. Small-scale user testing on our prototype has also begun (see Q10).

**3. Why is this problem important, and whom does it affect?**

The problem affects secondary school and university students who need support but have not accessed services, and it also costs professionals time: social workers and counsellors must reconstruct event context, duration and priorities in a first conversation. Importantly, many people do not realise their emotions are in trouble — distress accumulates gradually through everyday events, and people only notice when it becomes hard to bear, when the history is already difficult to reconstruct. Our answer is a fragmented, effortless recording method: tap one button, say a few sentences, no topics, no forms. When recording is that easy, people are more willing to record their lives, and AI can organise these fragments in the background, notice possible warning signs, and gently suggest talking to someone at the right moment.

**4. What existing solutions are available, and what are their limitations?**

Counsellors and social workers offer professional support, but students must find services, initiate contact and explain their experiences themselves. Helplines offer human contact, but limited availability and one-off calls restrict follow-up. AI mental-health chatbots are accessible but can misunderstand complex situations and cannot replace professional judgement. Ordinary diary or note apps preserve experiences, but users must still organise the material themselves before seeking help. Unfold combines free private recording, automatic organisation across records, calendar review, background support prompts and a human handoff to a social worker — a combination none of these alternatives provides on its own.

**5. Describe the main goals of your project.**

The main goal is to let students freely record everyday life, have the system organise it automatically, and give students a calendar with neutral daily summaries — with no psychological ratings, risk scores or analysis reports in the student interface. Recording is met only with brief acknowledgement, encouragement and an optional invitation to add context, keeping interaction low intensity. Only when background analysis judges that records approach a support threshold does the app give a short explanation and ask whether to submit a case. After the student consents, a professional case summary goes to a social worker automatically. Our prototype now demonstrates this full flow end to end against a real two-sided backend: an Express + SQLite server, and a separate web console where verified social workers claim cases from a matched queue and reply (a local simulation remains only as an offline demo fallback).

**6. What are the boundaries of your project?**

The product does not diagnose, treat, replace emergency services or make autonomous clinical decisions; it only organises authorised records and analyses support cues to decide when to offer a prompt. Cases are shared with a social worker only after explicit student consent, and the system never contacts anyone by itself. Students see no background psychological analysis; routine responses are limited to acknowledgement, encouragement and optional elaboration, and students can finish an entry without responding. The current "threshold" is a deliberately conservative pattern check (at least 3 entries across at least 2 days covering at least 2 difficult topics) and is labelled in the app as not a clinical judgement. Live institutional integration, professional staffing, real-data trials and safeguarding for minors are out of scope for this stage.

**7. What assumptions have you made about the problem and its context?**

Our key assumptions, each mapped to a validation method in the questionnaire and user tests: (1) speaking is easier than structured forms — tested via preference questions and recording completion rates; (2) clear privacy controls build trust — tested by asking participants to explain where data goes; (3) low-intensity responses help rather than disturb recording — tested by observing whether invitations are skipped without annoyance; (4) calendar review is convenient — tested by asking students to find a given day; (5) an understandable support prompt increases willingness to seek help — tested with preset scenarios; (6) cross-day summaries are useful to professionals — tested by having social workers review synthetic summaries; (7) private recording alone has continued-use value — tested through later trial retention. Any unsupported assumption triggers a defined fallback, e.g. prioritising text input.

**8. Who is your target audience or user group?**

Primary users are Hong Kong secondary school and university students — especially those who want to record experiences first, are considering seeking help, or want to prepare for a first professional conversation. Social workers and counsellors form the other side of the process and will give design feedback on summaries and handoffs. The two student groups differ in private space, support pathways and sharing expectations, so they need separate analysis and different consent and safeguarding arrangements; which group to recruit first will be decided in light of questionnaire responses.

**9. Who are your customers, meaning the groups that pay for the solution?**

Expected customers are schools, universities and youth service organisations that provide or commission student support services. No paying customers, partner organisations or pricing model have been confirmed at this stage. Future discussions with potential organisations must establish whether they have professionals available to take cases, which workflows they want to improve, and how much additional reading and responding they can absorb, alongside maintenance, cloud and staffing costs. Student usefulness and institutional willingness to adopt will be validated separately.

**10. How do you collect information from your target audience, including testing?**

Two channels are currently active. First, our questionnaire — covering help-seeking barriers, recording preferences, privacy understanding and willingness to share — has been piloted for comprehensibility and is now being distributed; sensitive questions are optional and no detailed private disclosure is requested. Second, small-scale user testing has begun on the working prototype: participants record freely in preset scenarios, experience the brief system responses, review daily summaries in the calendar, and respond to a simulated support prompt and submission choice. A built-in synthetic sample week lets sessions run without collecting real private data. We observe both task outcomes and whether participants can correctly explain where their data goes. Later, social workers will review synthetic summaries for professional usefulness.

**11. How is your team organised, and what is each member responsible for?**

We are a four-person team in two groups. Product and research: Member A owns user research — questionnaire design and distribution, user-test recruitment and facilitation, interviews and analysis; Member B owns product and content design — user flows and interface prototypes, key copy such as onboarding and privacy explanations, and synthetic test cases, and will liaise with social workers for professional feedback. Development: Member C owns the app frontend and architecture — the Expo framework, recording, calendar and all screens, local state and storage; Member D owns data processing and AI modules — transcription integration with fallbacks, on-device deidentification, topic and attribute classification, the support-prompt rule, and unit tests. Important decisions are confirmed by all four members.

**12. What communication methods do you use to ensure effective collaboration?**

We hold a weekly meeting that walks a shared task board item by item — every task has an owner, status, dependencies and completion criteria — and meetings focus on showing actual outputs (screens, test records, data) rather than verbal percentages. Day-to-day coordination happens in an instant-messaging group where blockers are raised immediately. Code collaboration runs through Git branches and pull requests that the two developers review for each other before merging. Design files, questionnaire versions and meeting notes live in a shared cloud drive under version control. Decisions affecting product direction — such as switching to cloud transcription — are written up as short decision records with options, reasons and remaining validation needs.

**13. What work has been completed so far?**

We have defined the problem and target users with literature support; designed the questionnaire and put it into distribution; and completed a working prototype (Expo, iOS/Android/web) covering the full flow: voice recording, ElevenLabs Scribe v2 transcription (Cantonese and English, converted to traditional Chinese), on-device deidentification — now covering English and common Chinese names, Chinese street addresses and estates, and Hong Kong and mainland phone formats — a calendar with neutral daily summaries, a background support-prompt rule, a pre-submission summary the student can preview and edit, and consent-based submission. The student side now runs against a real backend (Express + better-sqlite3, all GenAI calls server-side on Moonshot kimi-k3 with deterministic fallbacks, keys never in the client): de-identified entries sync under an independent cloud-organisation consent, with backfill of existing notes on opt-in, cloud copies purged on opt-out, and per-note and delete-everything removals propagated to the server. The social worker side is a separate web console with registration, verification gating, a language- and expertise-matched queue, claiming, replies, rematch and withdrawal; timeouts auto-return unresponsive cases to the queue. An "Ask your diary" search (server NL Q&A with an on-device fallback), sample-week demo data and delete-everything controls are included. The client carries 28 unit tests and the server 77, covering deidentification, classification, consent, sync, case transitions and fallback behaviour, and all pass. Questionnaire analysis and formal user evaluation are not yet complete.

**14. Which part of the project are you currently working on?**

Three lines in parallel: distributing the questionnaire and preparing the analysis plan; running small-scale user tests on the prototype; and technical consolidation. Two of the original four technical priorities have landed: GenAI classification now runs server-side for cross-record topic linking, event-time handling and uncertainty labels (with deterministic fallbacks when no key is configured), and deidentification now covers Chinese names, addresses and phone formats with conservative matching and boundary tests. The two remaining priorities are moving the ElevenLabs API key behind a small backend proxy (it is currently extractable from the client bundle — a documented known issue) and designing prompt thresholds and wording with professionals. We are also preparing the evaluation cases needed to measure transcription accuracy, summary fidelity and prompt quality.

**16. How will you evaluate whether the project is progressing successfully?**

We will measure: recording-task completion, time and abandonment reasons; transcription accuracy, empty-transcript rate and traditional-conversion correctness, including noisy Cantonese settings; students' understanding of what stays on-device, what goes to the cloud and what a social worker sees; whether responses stay brief and skippable; whether students can find and understand a daily summary; understanding of prompt explanations and submission scope; false/missed prompt rates against cases and criteria set with professionals; missed or over-removed identifiers (including Chinese) in synthetic cases; timeline, attribute and cross-record linking correctness; summary fidelity against source records; professional feedback on context completeness and reading burden; and willingness to use and share from the questionnaire and tests. Numerical targets will be fixed before evaluation. Satisfaction alone cannot prove adoption, and short trials cannot prove clinical effectiveness.

**17. What challenges have you encountered, and how have you addressed them?**

The hardest was speech-to-text accuracy in noisy environments. Students record in classrooms, corridors and on transport, and Cantonese colloquial speech adds difficulty; we saw wrong characters and even empty transcripts. We responded on three layers: capture optimisation (high-quality recording preset and audio-session configuration), noise reduction and audio pre-processing before transcription (with more systematic options under evaluation), and failure fallbacks (clear copy for empty or failed transcripts, switching to typing, and editing before saving). We also iterated through three on-device transcription approaches — Whisper, expo-speech-recognition and browser dictation — none of which met quality or stability needs, so we moved to ElevenLabs Scribe v2 in the cloud with explicit onboarding consent; the questionnaire tests whether students accept this trade-off. Other solved issues: simplified-to-traditional Chinese conversion (opencc-js), and documenting the exposed API key with a mitigation plan.

**18. What challenges do you anticipate, and how will you address them?**

The central anticipated challenge is guaranteeing the accuracy of AI judgement: prompts too early cause distress, too late are useless, and distorted summaries mislead professionals. Our response is layered defence. Stay conservative and transparent: the prompt rule is deliberately cautious and labelled non-clinical, and the student interface shows no ratings, so a wrong background judgement never labels the student. The AI never acts alone: it contacts no one by itself, submission needs student consent, and a professional ultimately reads the case. Calibrate with professionals: thresholds and acceptable error levels are set with social workers, not by developers alone. Evaluate systematically: synthetic cases covering mixed topics, edge cases and Chinese identifiers measure classification errors, summary fidelity and prompt false positives/misses, kept on record. Preserve uncertainty: summaries keep source excerpts and hedged wording. Our design assumption is that the cost of error is controlled, not that errors are zero. We also anticipate and plan for Cantonese transcription errors, missed Chinese identifiers, timeline confusion between recording time and event time, over-chatty responses, unclear retention and withdrawal rules, social worker capacity limits, and safeguarding requirements for minors.

**19. What resources have you used so far, and how have they helped?**

Published Hong Kong studies [1, 2] grounded our problem definition, and the Hong Kong privacy regulator's AI framework [3] guides our data-handling review. Technically we use: Expo / React Native (one codebase for iOS, Android and web, keeping maintenance affordable for a four-person team); ElevenLabs Scribe v2 for Cantonese-and-English transcription, adopted after three on-device attempts; opencc-js for simplified-to-traditional conversion; AsyncStorage for on-device persistence; an Express + better-sqlite3 backend with a React worker console, all GenAI calls held server-side on Moonshot kimi-k3; and TypeScript unit suites (28 client tests, 77 server tests) that let us change classification and deidentification logic safely. Questionnaire tooling supported design and distribution. These resources let a small team build and iterate a full-flow prototype within one term.

**20. What additional resources do you need?**

We need: more student participants to grow the questionnaire sample and later user-test rounds; social worker or counsellor feedback on summary fields, prompt thresholds and case acceptance flows; privacy, security and safeguarding advice, especially for minors, before any real-data trial; synthetic test cases covering mixed topics, earlier-event accounts, cross-record links and Chinese identifiers; a small backend proxy and a GenAI classification service (to fix key exposure and replace keyword rules), including a cost assessment for who operates them; and institutional partners able to host a supervised pilot with defined response arrangements. Named owners and outcomes for each will be reported in the next update.

**21. What new insights have you gained about the problem, technology or teamwork?**

On the problem: available services do not equal usable services — students need to see a purpose for recording, understand where data goes, and control disclosure; and because many do not notice their own emotional changes, low-barrier recording is itself part of the value. On technology: "everything stays on device" conflicts with Cantonese transcription quality, making cloud transcription with explicit consent the more honest compromise today; privacy promises must hold in both copy and code to survive scrutiny; and our automation has so far proved "it can be automatic", not yet "it organises correctly" — keyword rules close the demo loop but are not capability evidence. On teamwork: terms like "stays local" and "withdraw" had to become concrete data flows and state changes, or design and development would have interpreted them differently.

**22. How has your understanding of the problem changed?**

We now see the problem as two linked gaps: not only the difficulty of explaining accumulated experiences when seeking help, but also the earlier gap that many students never realise their distress deserves attention — which is why fragmented recording matters. Building the prototype also corrected several assumptions: transcription moved from on-device to cloud with informed consent; the student interface became a neutral calendar instead of visible classifications; the pre-submission step became a short editable two-sentence summary with expandable de-identified excerpts rather than either a full report or a blind one-tap choice; and withdrawal, rematching and messaging turned from vague concepts into explicit implemented states. These are clarified design decisions, not yet validated effects — the questionnaire, user tests and professional review will test them.
